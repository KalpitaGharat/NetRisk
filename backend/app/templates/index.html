The Problem in AI-IDS Engineering
  • ✕ Dogmatic SDLC selection based on habit rather than measurable risk.
  • ✕ AI/ML models suffer from adversarial evasion and model drift without iterative verification.
  • ✕ Rapid zero-day exploit mutation clashes with rigid, frozen requirement models.
  • ✕ High compliance penalties and false-positive spikes during post-deployment patching.

Seven Core Project Risk Dimensions

Normalized linear parameters ($F_i \in [0, 100]$) calibrated for cybersecurity and AI architectures
Weights Total: 100%
Security Criticality 25%

Defended asset sensitivity, confidentiality, and breach impact.

AI / ML Complexity 20%

Neural network architecture, model drift vulnerability, and retraining.

Threat Volatility 15%

Evolution speed of novel zero-day exploits and evasion vectors.

Requirement Uncertainty 15%

Ambiguity and fluctuation in functional detection requirements.

Operational Criticality 10%

Wire-speed inspection throughput and acceptable false-alarm tolerance.

Compliance Requirement 10%

Statutory compliance rigor (NIST SP 800-53, ISO 27001, GDPR).

Team Risk (Inverted) 5%

Inversely derived: $\text{Team Risk} = 100 - \text{Team Experience}$.

Five Supported SDLC Methodologies

Mathematically modeled without arbitrary bias or subjective favoritism

Waterfall Model

Excels when requirements and attack surfaces are stable, frozen, and clearly specified.

V-Model

Highest suitability for mission-critical security, rigorous verification, and statutory compliance.

Agile Framework

Favored when functional specifications and adversarial patterns are in constant flux.

Spiral Model

Optimal for deep learning research, experimental feature spaces, and risk-driven prototyping.

DevSecOps Paradigm

Best for cloud-native microservices requiring automated CI/CD security gating and rapid deployment.
